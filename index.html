The Hidden Cost of Artificial Intelligence: Energy, Water and Land Behind Every Prompt

Chill Guys: Francesco Frattini, Mattia Di Luca and Mohamad Jaffal

Data Visualization project, University of Genoa

Every answer from an AI system is produced in a data center, and every data center draws on electricity, water and land. This site asks what those costs are, how fast they are growing, and who bears them.

1 The project

1.1 Main question

What are the environmental costs of the infrastructure behind AI (energy, water and land), and who bears them?

Five supporting questions guide the visualizations:

  1. How much water does a single AI prompt use, and why do estimates differ so widely?
  2. How fast is data center electricity demand growing, and what do scenarios project for 2030–2035?
  3. Where are AI data centers concentrated, and which countries host them compared with those that benefit from them?
  4. Which regions are most exposed in terms of local water and energy pressure, and how do energy mixes differ across countries?
  5. How much do training runs of frontier models emit, and how is this changing over time?

1.2 Who this site is for

General readers who are curious and informed but not experts, such as students and citizens. Technical terms (data center, TWh, water withdrawal versus consumption) are explained in plain language, and uncertainty is always shown openly.

1.3 Data at hand

The charts published so far rely on three sources. Each chart states exactly which data it uses and what the data cannot tell.

  • IEA, Energy and AI: electricity consumption of data centers by world region in 2020–2024, with a Base Case projection to 2030. It covers all data centers, not only those used for AI.
  • Epoch AI, Frontier Data Centers: location, power, owners and users of large AI data centers, built from public sources and satellite imagery. It is a selection of the largest sites, not a census.
  • Ember, Yearly Electricity Data: electricity generation, mix and carbon intensity of national grids. It shows how clean the power is in the countries that host the data centers.

Further sources will be added in later pages, including the Stanford AI Index 2026, Google’s measurement of AI inference at scale, “Making AI Less Thirsty” and the Berkeley Lab report on US data centers.

Most figures on data centers do not separate AI workloads from other uses, and per-prompt water estimates vary with the model, the prompt and the location. The visualizations flag these limits where they matter.
